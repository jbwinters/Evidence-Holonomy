\title{Evidence Holonomy and Entropy Production:\\
From Universal Coding to Irreversibility}
\author{Joshua Winters\\Independent Researcher\\\texttt{josh@friendmachine.co}}
\date{August 27, 2025; revised September 25, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
We define an ``evidence holonomy'' functional on loops of representation transforms applied to sample paths, in two versions. \textbf{Representation-space holonomy} scores each representation with its own universal code; by pointwise universality it converges to the entropy-rate difference $h(Q)-h(P)$, where $Q$ is the law of the loop's output. \textbf{KL holonomy} instead scores the original path under the loop's pushforward law; its rate is the relative-entropy rate $\mathsf{d}(P\|Q)$, and for the time-reversal loop of a stationary finite-state Markov chain it equals the entropy production rate $\sigma$ (bits/step). Estimating KL holonomy requires a model of $Q$ that is accurate on paths typical for $P$, which universality alone does not provide. We analyze the order-$R$ plug-in estimator implemented in our code. It is consistent for $\sigma$ on Markov chains for every $R\ge 1$. On records observed through a memoryless channel, averages of its order-wise limits are lower bounds on the hidden entropy production. Numerical checks on random chains, closed-form rings, reversible chains, and a hidden ring show convergence, a small positive finite-sample bias, and the gap between observed and hidden irreversibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Setup and Definitions}

\paragraph{Alphabet and path space.} Fix a finite alphabet $\X$. Let $\X^n$ denote length-$n$ strings and $\X^{\N}$ the one-sided sequence space with its product $\sigma$-algebra. Let $P$ be a stationary ergodic probability measure on $\X^{\N}$. Write $X_{0:n-1}$ for the length-$n$ prefix of a sample from $P$ and $P_n$ for its law on $\X^n$.

\paragraph{Universal codes.}
A \emph{universal code} on alphabet $\mathcal{A}$ is a map $\code_{\mathcal{A}}:\bigcup_{n\ge 1}\mathcal{A}^n\to \RR_+$ assigning a code length in bits to any finite string, such that for every stationary ergodic law $Q$ on $\mathcal{A}^{\N}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:universality}
\frac{1}{n}\Big(\code_{\mathcal{A}}(Y_{0:n-1}) + \log_2 Q_n(Y_{0:n-1})\Big) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0
\quad\text{$Q$-a.s. and in } L^1(Q).
\end{equation}
Classical examples include LZ78, Krichevsky--Trofimov (KT) mixtures over Markov orders, and context-tree weighting \cite{ziv1978,kt1981,willems1995ctw,shields1996,csiszarshields2004}. Note that \eqref{eq:universality} is a statement about paths typical for $Q$ only.

\paragraph{Standing assumptions.}
Unless stated otherwise, alphabets are finite; processes are stationary and ergodic;
and when relative-entropy rates are finite we assume $P_n\ll Q_n$ for all $n$.
All logs are base 2, and all rates are per symbol.

\paragraph{Representation transforms and loops.}
For each $n$, let $F_{i,n}:\X_{i-1}^{\,n_{i-1}(n)}\to\X_i^{\,n_i(n)}$ be measurable, where the alphabets
$\X_i$ may differ by step and $n_0(n)=n$. A finite list $\gamma=(F_{1,n},\ldots,F_{m,n})$ is a \emph{loop at scale $n$}
if $\X_m=\X_0=\X$ and $n_m(n)=n+O(1)$. Write $x^{(i)} = F_{i,n}\circ\cdots\circ F_{1,n}(x)$ and $L_n=F_{m,n}\circ\cdots\circ F_{1,n}$.
When $n_m(n)\neq n$, the two arguments being compared are aligned by dropping $O(1)$ boundary symbols,
which does not affect per-symbol limits.

\paragraph{Why ``holonomy''?}
In differential geometry, holonomy measures the failure of a vector to return unchanged after
parallel transport around a closed loop. Here the ``vector'' is the description length (or log-likelihood)
an observer assigns to a path, and the holonomy is its net change after the path is carried around a loop
of representations. Bijective relabelings are ``flat'' (zero holonomy); time reversal of a nonequilibrium
process is not. The analogy is motivational: we use it to organize two different ways of comparing evidence
before and after a loop, not to claim a connection or curvature in the geometric sense.

\subsection{Two holonomy functionals}

\begin{definition}[Representation-space holonomy]\label{def:holonomy-out}
Given universal codes $\code_{\X_i}$ for the intermediate alphabets,
\[
\mathrm{Hol}_{n}^{\mathrm{out},\gamma}(x)
  = \sum_{i=1}^m \Big(\code_{\X_i}\big(x^{(i)}\big)-\code_{\X_{i-1}}\big(x^{(i-1)}\big)\Big)
  = \code_{\X}\big(L_n(x)\big)-\code_{\X}(x).
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[KL holonomy]\label{def:holonomy-kl}
Let $Q_n=(L_n)\push P_n$ be the law of the loop's output. The (ideal) KL holonomy is
\[
\mathrm{Hol}^{\mathrm{KL},\gamma}_n(x) \;=\; \log_2 \frac{P_n(x)}{Q_n(x)},
\]
the code length of $x$ under $Q_n$ minus its code length under $P_n$. Its expectation is
$\E_P[\mathrm{Hol}^{\mathrm{KL},\gamma}_n] = \KL(P_n\|Q_n)$.
\end{definition}

Definition~\ref{def:holonomy-kl} involves the unknown laws $P_n$ and $Q_n$; Section~\ref{sec:estimation} discusses how to estimate it from data.

\section{Reductions}

\begin{lemma}[Representation-space holonomy]\label{lem:out}
Suppose the loop's outputs are the $n$-marginals of a stationary ergodic process $Q$ (see Section~\ref{sec:stationary}), and $\code_\X$ is universal. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:hol-out-reduction}
\frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathrm{Hol}_{n}^{\mathrm{out},\gamma}(X_{0:n-1}) - \log_2\frac{P_n(X_{0:n-1})}{Q_n(L_n(X_{0:n-1}))}\Big) \xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{} 0 \quad\text{in } L^1(P).
\end{equation}
If, in addition, the outputs $L_n(X_{0:n-1})$ are prefixes of a single $Q$-distributed sequence (as for sliding-block codes), the convergence also holds $P$-a.s.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $L_n(X_{0:n-1})\sim Q_n$, the $L^1(Q)$ part of \eqref{eq:universality} applied to $Q$ controls $\code_\X(L_n(X))+\log_2 Q_n(L_n(X))$, and applied to $P$ controls $\code_\X(X)+\log_2 P_n(X)$; subtract \cite{barron1985,shields1996}. $L^1$ convergence depends only on the marginal laws. Almost-sure convergence needs the outputs for different $n$ to lie on one sample path. This fails for time reversal, where $L_n(x)$ is a different string for every $n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Expectation-level identities]\label{thm:exp_reductions}
Under the assumptions of Lemma~\ref{lem:out},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exp-out}
\frac{1}{n}\,\E_P\big[\mathrm{Hol}_{n}^{\mathrm{out},\gamma}\big] = h(Q)-h(P)+o(1).
\end{equation}
For KL holonomy, whenever the limit exists,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:exp-kl}
\frac{1}{n}\,\E_P\big[\mathrm{Hol}_{n}^{\mathrm{KL},\gamma}\big]
= \frac{1}{n}\,\KL(P_n\|Q_n)\;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\; \mathsf{d}(P\|Q)\;\ge 0.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take expectations in \eqref{eq:hol-out-reduction}, using $\E_P[-\log_2 P_n(X)]=H(P_n)$ and $\E_P[-\log_2 Q_n(L_n(X))]=H(Q_n)$, and divide by $n$. The second identity is Definition~\ref{def:holonomy-kl}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
Equation~\eqref{eq:exp-out} measures net compression or expansion under the loop and does not depend on which universal code is used. Equation~\eqref{eq:exp-kl} is the \emph{irreversibility} functional: it compares two laws on the same coordinates and is non-negative.
\end{remark}

\section{Canonical Loops}

\subsection{Gauge invariance for bijective loops}

\begin{corollary}[Gauge invariance]\label{cor:gauge}
If the loop is the identity map on $\X^n$ (for example, a relabeling followed by its inverse), then $Q_n=P_n$, so $\mathrm{Hol}_{n}^{\mathrm{KL},\gamma}\equiv 0$ and $\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Hol}_{n}^{\mathrm{out},\gamma}(X_{0:n-1}) = 0$.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Coarse-graining and observation channels}\label{sec:stationary}

A deterministic coarse-graining followed by a lift, $L_n=R_n\circ K_n$, produces outputs supported on the image of $R_n$. Unless $P_n$ is supported there too, $P_n\not\ll Q_n$ and the KL rate is infinite. So deterministic coarse-grain--lift loops do not give informative KL holonomies. (Estimators with smoothed probabilities return finite numbers that grow as the smoothing vanishes.)

The useful statement about coarse-graining concerns applying the \emph{same} channel to both laws. Let $C$ be a memoryless channel (a stochastic matrix applied independently to each symbol), and write $CP$ for the law of the channel output when the input has law $P$. By the data-processing inequality \cite{coverthomas},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dpi}
\KL\big((CP)_n\,\|\,(CQ)_n\big) \le \KL(P_n\|Q_n)\quad\text{for every } n.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{When is the pushforward stationary?}
For a general sequence of maps $L_n$, the laws $Q_n$ need not be the $n$-marginals of any stationary process.
A sufficient condition is that the loop acts as a shift-commuting sliding-block code $(L_n(x))_t = F(x_{t-m:t+m})$.
Time reversal is not a sliding-block code, but the reversed process of a stationary process is stationary, so it is covered directly.

\subsection{Time reversal and entropy production for Markov chains}

Let $P$ be a stationary Markov chain on $\X=\{1,\dots,k\}$ with transition matrix $T$ and stationary law $\pi$. Its time reversal $P^{\mathrm{rev}}$ is the Markov chain with transitions $T^\ast_{ij}=\pi_j T_{ji}/\pi_i$; equivalently, $P^{\mathrm{rev}}_n(x_0,\dots,x_{n-1})=P_n(x_{n-1},\dots,x_0)$.

\paragraph{Time-reversal loop.}
The loop is literal reversal, $L_n(x_{0:n-1})=(x_{n-1},\dots,x_0)$, so $(L_n)\push P_n=P^{\mathrm{rev}}_n$. It can also be written as a loop through the transition alphabet $\X^2$. Encode $x$ as its edge sequence $e_t=(x_{t-1},x_t)$, $t=1,\dots,n-1$; reverse the edge sequence; then keep the \emph{second} state of each edge. This yields $(x_{n-1},x_{n-2},\dots,x_1)$, the reversal of $x_{1:n-1}$, which differs from literal reversal by one boundary symbol. Our implementation uses this form and compares it against $x_{1:n-1}$. The edge presentation adds no information beyond literal reversal.

\begin{theorem}[KL holonomy of time reversal equals entropy production]\label{thm:EP}
Suppose $T_{ij}>0 \Leftrightarrow T_{ji}>0$ for all $i,j$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EP_rate}
\KL(P_n\|P^{\mathrm{rev}}_n) = (n-1)\,\sigma,\qquad
\sigma = \sum_{i,j}\pi_i T_{ij}\,\log_2\frac{\pi_i T_{ij}}{\pi_j T_{ji}}\quad(\text{bits/step}),
\end{equation}
so $\mathsf{d}(P\|P^{\mathrm{rev}})=\sigma$ and $\frac{1}{n}\E_P[\mathrm{Hol}_{n}^{\mathrm{KL},\mathrm{rev}}]\to\sigma$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a path $x=x_{0:n-1}$ with positive probability,
\[
\log_2\frac{P_n(x)}{P^{\mathrm{rev}}_n(x)}=\log_2\frac{P_n(x_{0:n-1})}{P_n(x_{n-1},\dots,x_0)}
= \log_2\frac{\pi_{x_0}}{\pi_{x_{n-1}}} + \sum_{t=1}^{n-1}\log_2\frac{T_{x_{t-1}x_t}}{T_{x_t x_{t-1}}}.
\]
Under stationarity, the boundary term has expectation zero and each summand has expectation $\sum_{i,j}\pi_iT_{ij}\log_2(T_{ij}/T_{ji})$. This equals $\sigma$ because $\sum_{i,j}\pi_iT_{ij}\log_2(\pi_i/\pi_j)=\sum_i\pi_i\log_2\pi_i-\sum_j\pi_j\log_2\pi_j=0$. This is the standard identification of entropy production with the relative entropy between forward and reversed path measures \cite{schnakenberg1976,crooks1999,kawai2007,seifert2012}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Absolute continuity]
If $T_{ij}>0$ but $T_{ji}=0$ for some pair, then $P_n\not\ll P^{\mathrm{rev}}_n$ and the rate is $+\infty$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why not $h(Q)-h(P)$?]
Reversal preserves entropy rates, $h(P)=h(P^{\mathrm{rev}})$, so representation-space holonomy vanishes for the time-reversal loop. Only the KL version detects irreversibility.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{General stationary processes.} For any stationary $P$ with $P_n\ll P^{\mathrm{rev}}_n$, the same definitions give $\frac{1}{n}\E_P[\mathrm{Hol}_{n}^{\mathrm{KL},\mathrm{rev}}]\to\mathsf{d}(P\|P^{\mathrm{rev}})$ whenever this limit exists.

\section{Estimation}\label{sec:estimation}

\paragraph{Universality is not enough.} A natural idea is to estimate $\log_2 Q_n(x)$ with a universal code trained on samples from $Q$. But \eqref{eq:universality} controls code lengths only on paths typical for $Q$. When $P\neq Q$ are both stationary ergodic, they are mutually singular, and a $P$-typical path is $Q$-atypical. So universality says nothing about the code length a $Q$-trained coder assigns to $X\sim P$. Code-length \emph{differences} from separate compressors (for example, LZ78 on $x$ and on $L_n(x)$) estimate $h(Q)-h(P)$, which is zero for reversal. Estimating $\mathsf{d}(P\|Q)$ requires conditional probabilities of $Q$ that are accurate on contexts that occur under $P$.

\paragraph{The plug-in estimator.} Given $x_{0:n-1}$, let $y=x_{1:n-1}$ and $\tilde y=(x_{n-1},\dots,x_1)$ its reversal. For $r=0,\dots,R$, let $\hat p_r(a\mid c)$ be the KT estimate $(N_y(c,a)+\tfrac12)/(N_y(c)+\tfrac k2)$ from the counts of context $c\in\X^r$ followed by $a$ in $y$, and $\hat q_r$ the same from $\tilde y$. Mix orders with the Bayesian posterior weights obtained by sequentially coding $y$ (respectively $\tilde y$), then freeze the resulting predictors $\hat p$ and $\hat q$. The estimate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:estimator}
\hat\sigma_{n,R} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t}\log_2\frac{\hat p(y_t\mid y_{t-R:t-1})}{\hat q(y_t\mid y_{t-R:t-1})},
\end{equation}
where the first few symbols use shorter contexts. Both predictors are evaluated on the same $y$ used to fit $\hat p$ (an in-sample plug-in estimate, cf.\ \cite{roldanparrondo2010}).

\begin{proposition}[Consistency for Markov chains]\label{prop:consistency}
Let $P$ be a stationary irreducible Markov chain with $T_{ij}>0\Leftrightarrow T_{ji}>0$, and let $R\ge 1$. Then $\hat\sigma_{n,R}\to\sigma$ almost surely.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By the ergodic theorem, the empirical frequencies of $(r{+}1)$-blocks in $y$ converge a.s.\ to those of $P$. Those in $\tilde y$ are the same blocks reversed, so they converge to those of $P^{\mathrm{rev}}$. Both $P$ and $P^{\mathrm{rev}}$ are first-order Markov. So for every $r\ge1$ and every context of positive probability, $\hat p_r(\cdot\mid c)\to T_{c_{r},\cdot}$ and $\hat q_r(\cdot\mid c)\to T^\ast_{c_{r},\cdot}$, and the KT smoothing vanishes. If $T$ is not rank one, the order-0 component's log-likelihood deficit grows linearly in $n$, so its posterior weight tends to zero. If $T$ is rank one, the chain is i.i.d., $\sigma=0$, and every component converges to $\pi$. Hence $\hat p\to T$ and $\hat q\to T^\ast$ uniformly over the finitely many contexts visited. By the support condition, $T^\ast_{ij}>0$ whenever $T_{ij}>0$, so the log-ratios stay bounded. The ergodic theorem applied to \eqref{eq:estimator} gives the limit $\sum_{i,j}\pi_iT_{ij}\log_2(T_{ij}/T^\ast_{ij})=\sigma$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Robustness to $R$, not coder independence]
Every order $r\ge1$ is consistent for a first-order chain, and the posterior concentrates on the smallest adequate order. So estimates for different $R\ge1$ nearly coincide at moderate $n$; Section~\ref{sec:numerics} reports agreement to within $10^{-13}$ bits/step. This reflects a shared, correctly specified model class. It is not evidence that arbitrary universal codes yield the same KL estimate.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Observed records bound hidden entropy production]\label{prop:hidden}
Let $Z$ be a stationary Markov chain satisfying the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:EP}, with entropy production $\sigma_Z$, and let $Y=CZ$ be observed through a memoryless channel $C$. Write $D_m=\KL(P^Y_m\|P^{Y,\mathrm{rev}}_m)$. Then $D_m\le(m-1)\sigma_Z$ for every $m$, so $\mathsf{d}(P^Y\|P^{Y,\mathrm{rev}})\le\sigma_Z$ whenever the rate exists.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Because the channel acts independently on each symbol, reversing the observed path is the same as observing the reversed hidden path: $P^{Y,\mathrm{rev}}_m=(C P^{Z,\mathrm{rev}})_m$. Apply \eqref{eq:dpi} and Theorem~\ref{thm:EP}.
\end{proof}

For an observed process $Y$, Proposition~\ref{prop:consistency}'s argument shows that the order-$R$ estimator converges (under ergodicity and positivity) to the conditional quantity $d_R = D_{R+1}-D_R$. Because $D_1=0$ (reversal preserves one-symbol marginals), the averages satisfy
\[
\frac{1}{m-1}\sum_{R=1}^{m-1} d_R \;=\; \frac{D_m}{m-1}\;\le\;\sigma_Z .
\]
Individual terms $d_R$ need not be monotone in $R$ or below $\sigma_Z$. So a single finite-order estimate is not a certified bound, and the in-sample bias below pushes it upward.

\begin{remark}[Finite-sample behavior]
Evaluating $\hat p$ in-sample favors the forward model, so $\hat\sigma_{n,R}$ has a positive bias. A heuristic $\chi^2$ argument suggests it is of order $k^{R'+1}/n$, with $R'$ the order the mixture selects; for reversible chains ($\sigma=0$), Section~\ref{sec:numerics} shows it shrinking roughly in proportion to $1/n$. Fluctuations are of order $n^{-1/2}$. The code provides a block bootstrap for intervals.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Reversible chains and bias.} For 10 random detailed-balance chains of each size ($\sigma=0$), the mean estimate with $R=3$ is 0.0015 bits/step at $n=2^{11}$ and 0.00013 at $n=2^{15}$ for 3-state chains, and 0.0100 and 0.00072 for 5-state chains. Estimates with $R=1$ and $R=3$ are identical. The bias shrinks by a factor of 12--14 over a 16-fold increase in $n$, consistent with the roughly $1/n$ behavior described in Section~\ref{sec:estimation}.

\paragraph{Order robustness and closed forms.} On first-order chains, estimates with $R=1,2,3,5$ agree to within $10^{-13}$ bits/step, as the remark in Section~\ref{sec:estimation} predicts. The validation battery (\texttt{uec-battery --run\_suite}) also checks:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.25em,itemsep=0.1em]
\item three-state rings with closed-form $\sigma$, to within 5\% relative error at $n=1.5\times10^5$;
\item a reversible two-state chain (estimate below $5\times10^{-4}$);
\item gauge loops (below $5\times10^{-3}$);
\item agreement with a smoothed transition-count estimator;
\item block-bootstrap coverage of $\sigma$;
\item negative controls: alphabet mismatch, empty outputs, and self-evidence differences, which stay near zero under reversal even when $\sigma>0$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Hidden-state records.} Figure~\ref{fig:hmm} observes a three-state ring ($p=0.3$ forward, $q=0.05$ backward, $\sigma_Z=0.646$ bits/step) through two channels, for $n=2^{17}$ and three seeds. Through a noisy channel that reports the true state with probability $0.8$, the estimates for $R=1,\dots,6$ range from 0.085 to 0.127 bits/step, well below $\sigma_Z$. They rise from $R=1$ to $R=3$ and then stay the same: at this sample size the mixture puts negligible weight on orders above 3, so larger $R$ adds nothing. Through a channel that merges states 1 and 2, the observation is symmetric under the relabeling that maps the ring to its reversal. The observed process is therefore exactly reversible, and the estimates stay at most $7\times10^{-5}$ bits/step even though $\sigma_Z>0$.

\section*{Discussion}
The two holonomies answer different questions. Evaluating each representation with its own universal code gives the entropy-rate difference $h(Q)-h(P)$: it is insensitive to the choice of code and detects net compression or expansion, but it vanishes for time reversal. Evaluating the original path under the loop's pushforward law gives the relative-entropy rate $\mathsf{d}(P\|Q)$, which for Markov time reversal is the entropy production rate. The second quantity is the one that captures irreversibility. It requires a consistent model of the reversed process on forward-typical contexts, and a finite-order plug-in estimator supplies one for finite-state Markov chains.

\paragraph{Extensions.} Quantizing a continuous process and observing a Markov chain through a memoryless channel are both instances of \eqref{eq:dpi}. The observed irreversibility rate therefore bounds the underlying entropy production from below \cite{gomezmarin2008}; the quantum analogue follows from the monotonicity of quantum relative entropy \cite{lindblad1975,uhlmann1977}. Turning finite-sample, finite-order estimates into certified bounds would require controlling both the in-sample bias and the order-wise terms $d_R$.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The results assume finite alphabets, stationarity, and ergodicity. The consistency result covers finite-state Markov chains; for hidden-state records, the estimator targets the conditional quantities $d_R$, not $\sigma_Z$. Estimates carry a positive finite-sample bias (empirically of order $1/n$) and $O(n^{-1/2})$ fluctuations. The loop formalism organizes these estimators but does not by itself supply new estimators for loops other than time reversal.
